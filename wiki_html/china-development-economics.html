Political Economy

China Development Economics

China's development model as a specific political-economic system: central coordination plus aggressive local execution. Local governments control land, finance, and implementation capacity, while the center steers incentives, manages imbalances, and keeps national coherence.

01

Core Governance Model

China is a five-level governing system:

Central Government
Province
City
County / District
Township / Village

Two structural tensions dominate the system: the center must preserve unity, while local governments must run day-to-day operations because the country is too large to govern purely from above.

Three Governance Constraints

  • Externalities — cross-region spillovers push authority upward
  • Economies of scale — some services belong centrally, others locally
  • Information asymmetry — higher levels never fully know local reality

Result: Structural Tensions

  • Duplicated bureaucracies at each level
  • Overlapping reporting lines
  • Constant tension between autonomy and control
  • Local implementation capacity far exceeds local fiscal autonomy
02

Fiscal Architecture

The 1994 tax reform is the turning point for the modern fiscal model. Its effects:

  • Central government captured a much larger share of tax revenue
  • Local governments kept most expenditure responsibilities
  • The resulting gap had to be filled through transfers and alternative funding tools

Before 1994

  • Local governments retained larger revenue shares
  • Fragmented tax systems
  • Fiscal decentralization without uniform rules

After 1994 Reform

  • Money flows upward (central revenue share rises)
  • Responsibilities flow downward (local expenditure stays large)
  • Classic vertical imbalance
  • Land finance becomes the primary workaround

Land Finance

Local governments could convert rural land, transfer urban land-use rights, and use rising land values to fund development. That tied local public finance tightly to:

  • Property prices
  • Urban expansion
  • Industrial land allocation

The result was a growth engine, but also a structural dependence on real estate and land appreciation.

03

Local Government as Investor

Because local governments historically could not borrow directly from banks, they built local government financing vehicles (LGFVs / city investment companies). These entities typically had:

  • Land as collateral
  • Weak standalone economics
  • Implicit government guarantees

They funded infrastructure, industrial parks, and PPP-style projects. After the 2008 stimulus, these vehicles multiplied rapidly and debt expanded with them.

!
This is treated as both rational (many infrastructure projects have low direct ROI but large indirect benefits) and dangerous (implicit guarantees distort credit pricing and hide risk).

Political Incentives and Corruption

Local officials operate on short time horizons — mayors and party secretaries often have only a few years in office, while infrastructure projects take longer to mature. That creates a bias toward front-loaded investment, visible construction, land sales, and debt-funded expansion.

Corruption is treated here not as a side issue but as a structural byproduct of land, permits, financing, and government-business coordination. The land-development nexus became a major corruption channel in the 2008–2013 period.

04

Industrial Policy in Practice

Government support can work when four conditions hold: the domestic market is large enough, competition is preserved, subsidies are not mistaken for permanent protection, and failed firms are allowed to restructure or die.

Case StudyWhat WorkedWhat Didn't
BOE / LCDs State-linked investors provided patient financing. Multiple cities competed to host expansion. Early losses tolerated for strategic capability. High upfront cost; success required sustained political commitment across administrations
Photovoltaics Subsidies accelerated scale and cost reduction; China now dominates global solar manufacturing Local duplication created overcapacity; export dependence makes firms vulnerable to foreign policy shifts
Guidance Funds More venture-style evolution; government acts as LP or fund-of-funds; targets strategic technologies Incentive design is difficult; outside major cities, selection quality degrades

Key mechanisms producing inequality:

  • Hukou restricts access to local public services (education, healthcare)
  • Land quotas distort housing supply across regions
  • Top-tier cities face severe demand-supply imbalance
  • Household balance sheets become increasingly real-estate-heavy

Better growth now requires factor-market reform: freer labor mobility, more flexible land use, less local monopoly over land conversion.

06

Debt and Domestic Imbalance

Risk Composition

By 2018, total debt is high by emerging-market standards, but composition matters more than the headline number:

  • Very high corporate debt
  • SOE debt expansion after 2008
  • Real-estate developer leverage
  • Bank-led shadow banking
  • Hidden local-government debt through financing platforms

China did not mainly borrow to support consumption (as in the US mortgage cycle). It borrowed heavily to sustain investment, infrastructure, property, and local growth targets.

Domestic and International Imbalance

The core logic of domestic imbalance producing trade tension:

  • Household consumption is too low as a share of GDP
  • Investment is too high
  • Income distribution and weak social services increase precautionary saving
  • Excess production must be exported
★
Dual circulation is the proposed answer: stronger domestic demand and income growth, plus continued openness, but less reliance on external demand as the main absorber of overcapacity.
07

The Larger Thesis

China's development model succeeded because it combined: centralized strategic coordination, decentralized local competition, and a professional bureaucracy with strong execution capacity.

Production-Oriented Government (Past)

  • Optimized for industrialization
  • Construction-led growth
  • Production quantity over services
  • Land and investment as primary levers

Services-Oriented Government (Required)

  • Services over manufacturing
  • Redistribution and urban integration
  • Risk control over growth at any cost
  • Long-term innovation capacity
